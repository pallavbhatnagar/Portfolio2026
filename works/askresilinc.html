Overview

Research on why some AI assistants feel natural to talk to, what studies show actually works, and what designers and developers can change to make an AI talk better.

The short answer

An AI feels natural because of four things: how it was trained, the written rules it follows, what it remembers, and the features around it. If you use another company's AI, you control the last three. That is enough to fix most robotic behaviour.

There is one catch. Research shows that making an AI friendlier can make it get facts wrong more often 15. So every change to tone must be tested for accuracy too.

The twelve most important things I learned, with the evidence and what to do. Start here if you only have five minutes.

Why it matters: adoption and valuePeople keep using an AI assistant only if it is quick to get value from and easy to trust. Making Ask Resilinc talk naturally removes friction and builds the right amount of trust, and that is what turns a first try into everyday use.5 min

The short version

Most people already use AI, but far fewer trust it. In a 2025 study of over 48,000 people in 47 countries, 66% used AI regularly but only 46% were willing to trust it 84. For an AI assistant inside a product, that gap is the difference between a feature people try once and a feature they rely on.

How the assistant talks is a big part of whether people trust it. Robotic, repetitive or confusing replies make a capable assistant feel unreliable. Natural, direct and honest replies make it feel like a colleague worth asking again.

Why it matters

People adopt what is useful and easy
The most widely used model of technology adoption shows that people take up a tool when they find it useful and easy to use 87. A natural assistant improves both: answers come faster and are easier to act on, and nobody has to learn a special way of asking.
Satisfaction drives repeat use
Studies of AI service assistants find that how satisfied people are with the conversation predicts whether they keep using it 90.
One visible mistake costs more than it seems
People stop trusting an algorithm quickly after seeing it make a mistake, even when it outperforms people on average 88. Glitches like duplicated text or numbers that don't add up do more damage than their size suggests.
Trust needs to be right-sized, not maximised
Too little trust and people don't use the tool; too much and they stop checking it 89. 66% of people rely on AI output without checking its accuracy, and 56% have made mistakes at work because of AI 84. In one study, consultants using AI did better on tasks the AI was good at, but worse on tasks beyond its abilities 86. So "humanizing" must include being honest about uncertainty, not just sounding friendly.
AI helps less experienced people most
When customer support agents got an AI assistant, productivity rose 14% on average and 34% for newer, less skilled staff 85. An assistant that is easy to talk to can widen use beyond expert users.
Natural is not the same as human-like
Bots that act human make angry customers angrier 10. The goal is an assistant that responds well, not one that pretends to be a person.

What I looked at

AI assistants that people talk to in writing, such as chatbots and work assistants. I looked at how the big AI assistants are built, what published research says, and what designers and developers can change. Voice assistants and on-screen characters are only included where they teach me something about written conversation.

How much to trust each finding

  • Strong: several studies or lots of real-world data agree.
  • Moderate: one good study, or consistent company documents.
  • Early: one example or announcement. Useful, but not proof.

Every finding names its source, so you can check it yourself.

Limits

AI products change quickly, so anything about ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Copilot reflects what was public at the time of writing. Companies don't share everything about how they build their AI, so I rely on what they have chosen to publish.

ChatGPT

What stands out OpenAI publishes a rulebook for how ChatGPT should behave, including whose instructions win when they clash 37. Developers using its AI can set answer length separately from the content 41.

What went wrong In April 2025 an update made ChatGPT far too flattering. OpenAI rolled it back and said it had relied too much on quick thumbs-up ratings 38.

What to borrow Write the AI's rules down. Don't judge success on thumbs-up alone.

Claude

What stands out Anthropic trains character directly, aiming for traits like curiosity, directness and honesty 33. It publishes the instructions its apps give Claude, which show real tone and layout rules 35. Its styles feature lets users switch between preset or custom styles mid-conversation 34. Its researchers have also shown that traits like flattery can be measured inside the AI and watched over time 32.

What to borrow Describe personality as specific traits with reasons. Use the published instructions as real-world examples.

Gemini

What stands out Google says it coached Gemini with example answers and people's ratings 53. In 2025 Gemini added "personal context", drawing on past chats, switched on by default, plus a Temporary Chat mode that does not save or learn 42.

What to borrow If the AI remembers things, give people an easy way to say "don't remember this chat".

Microsoft Copilot

What stands out Microsoft 365 Copilot answers by looking up a company's own files, emails and calendars, rather than being retrained on them 54. The October 2025 update added long-term memory, an optional animated character called Mico, and a Real Talk style designed to challenge the user's assumptions instead of simply agreeing 43.

What to borrow Let the AI look up company documents to answer. Offer a candid mode that pushes back. Make characters and faces optional, since human-like touches can annoy frustrated users 10.

Patterns across all four

  1. Style is becoming a setting. Every product now lets users shape tone or length, which suggests the AI guessing your style is not enough on its own.
  2. Memory is an app feature with controls. None of them retrain the AI on your chats; they save notes and bring them back.
  3. Agreeing too easily is a known problem. OpenAI's rollback, Anthropic's character design and Copilot's Real Talk all respond to the same problem 14.
  4. The rules are written in plain language. OpenAI's rulebook and Anthropic's published instructions are the closest public examples of my instruction file.
Lessons from real productsReal stories show where AI conversations go wrong: being too agreeable, too human, or too sure of itself.2 min

ChatGPT became too flattering (2025)

What happened An April 2025 update made ChatGPT praise almost anything, including harmful ideas. OpenAI rolled it back within days, explaining it had relied too much on quick thumbs-up ratings 38.

Lesson Thumbs-up ratings reward flattery. Check accuracy and honesty too.

Warmer AI made more mistakes (2025)

What happened Oxford researchers trained five AIs to be warmer. They got facts wrong 10 to 30% more often, and agreed more with false beliefs, especially when people said they were sad 15.

Lesson Facts and risk levels must not change with tone. Test accuracy whenever tone changes.

Angry customers punished human-like bots (2022)

What happened In about 35,000 real customer chats and four experiments, chatbots that acted human made angry customers less satisfied. Acting human raised expectations the bot couldn't meet 10.

Lesson Use less personality during complaints, and be clear up front about what the bot can do.

An airline was liable for its chatbot (2024)

What happened Air Canada's chatbot gave a customer wrong information about a bereavement discount. A Canadian tribunal held the airline responsible and ordered it to pay 44.

Lesson Answers about rules and prices must come from official documents, not the AI's general knowledge.

A support bot invented a policy (2025)

What happened The support assistant for the coding tool Cursor told users about a login policy that did not exist, prompting complaints before the company corrected it 50.

Lesson When the agent does not know, it must say so.

Long chats made Bing drift (2023)

What happened In very long conversations, the early Bing chat assistant started acting strangely. Microsoft limited conversation length while it addressed the problem 49.

Lesson Test the AI in long conversations, not just single replies.

Google's phone AI sounded too human (2018)

What happened Google's Duplex booked appointments by phone with natural pauses and "um"s. Public objection that people did not know they were talking to a machine led Google to commit to disclosure 45.

Lesson Natural is good; passing as human is not. Disclosure is now a legal requirement in the EU 46.

Efficiency figures hid a quality problem (2025)

What happened Klarna promoted its AI assistant as doing the work of hundreds of agents, then said in 2025 that the focus on cost had lowered quality, and began hiring people again 51.

Lesson Measure how good the conversations are, not just how fast or how many.

How AI learns and remembersWhat studies show about how AI learns to talk, keeps track of conversations and makes mistakes, and what I can do with each finding.3 min

Coaching matters more than size (2022)

Source OpenAI study of ChatGPT's predecessor 11.

Finding People preferred answers from a small AI coached with people's ratings over an AI more than 100 times bigger without that coaching.

Apply it If I can't retrain the AI, I can copy the behaviour coaching produces by writing clear rules and showing examples.

A written list of values can coach an AI (2022)

Source Anthropic's "Constitutional AI" study 12.

Finding Giving the AI a short list of principles and having it check its own answers against them improved its behaviour, with far less human effort.

Apply it Write my rules as principles with reasons. The same rules can be used to automatically review replies.

AI can learn from "better vs worse" pairs (2023)

Source Stanford study on a simpler training method, called DPO 13.

Finding Showing the AI pairs of answers, one better and one worse, teaches it well, and is simpler than older methods.

Apply it If I ever retrain, collect pairs of better and worse answers rather than just scores. Some AI companies offer this as a service 52.

AI tells people what they want to hear (2023)

Source Anthropic study of five AI assistants 14.

Finding All five changed correct answers when users pushed back. People's own ratings rewarded agreeable answers, which is how the habit formed.

Apply it Tell the AI to stand by correct answers, and always test what happens when a user wrongly disagrees.

Friendlier AI makes more mistakes (2025)

Source Oxford study, later published in Nature 15.

Finding Five AIs trained to be warmer got facts wrong 10 to 30% more often and agreed more with false beliefs. Just asking for warmth in the instructions had a smaller but similar effect.

Apply it Any change to tone, including my instruction file, must be tested for accuracy before release.

AI learns to write too much (2023)

Source Study of how AI coaching rewards length 16.

Finding Much of the "improvement" from coaching came simply from answers getting longer.

Apply it Give the AI clear length rules for each kind of question, and use length settings where available 41.

AI skims the middle of long texts (2024)

Source "Lost in the middle" study 20.

Finding AI used information best when it was at the start or end of a long text, and worst when it was in the middle.

Apply it Keep instructions short and near the start. Sum up long conversations instead of sending them in full.

AI struggles over many messages (2025)

Source Microsoft and Salesforce study of 15 AIs 21.

Finding When a task was spread over several messages, AI did 39% worse on average. It guessed too early and stuck with wrong guesses.

Apply it When a request is missing key details, ask or state assumptions first. Before a final answer, sum up what the user has asked for.

AI can be taught whose instructions come first (2024)

Source OpenAI study on ranking instructions 17.

Finding AI trained to put the company's instructions above the user's, and both above text in documents, was harder to trick.

Apply it Keep my rules in the AI's main instructions. Never let documents or emails give it orders.

Examples teach quickly (2020)

Source OpenAI's GPT-3 study 18; Anthropic's writing guide for AI instructions 36.

Finding AI picks up a pattern from just a few examples, without retraining.

Apply it Include a few good and bad example replies in the instruction file, varied so it doesn't copy one template.

Looking things up makes AI more accurate (2020)

Source Study of AI that searches documents before answering (called RAG) 19.

Finding Giving the AI relevant documents to read before answering made its answers more accurate and specific.

Apply it Let the AI look up the organisation's documents for anything about the organisation. Tell it to say when the documents don't answer the question.

AI makes things up because tests reward guessing (2025)

Source OpenAI study 27; Anthropic study on AI knowing what it knows 28.

Finding AI often has some sense of when it's unsure, but tests that give no credit for "I don't know" teach it to guess confidently.

Apply it In my tests, score an honest "I'm not sure, here's what I can confirm" above a confident wrong answer.

Asking back only when needed works best (2022)

Source Study on when AI should ask clarifying questions 29.

Finding Asking a question back only when a request was genuinely unclear improved accuracy, without annoying users by asking every time.

Apply it Ask only when different readings would lead to different answers. Otherwise answer and say what you assumed.

Good memory means saving notes and picking the right ones (2023)

Source Stanford's "Generative Agents" study 23; "MemGPT" study 22.

Finding AI stayed consistent over time when the app saved notes and brought back the most relevant, recent and important ones.

Apply it Build memory as short saved notes, picked for each message, with a way for users to see and delete them.

How people react to AIWhat studies of human conversation, trust and chatbots tell me about designing AI that people find natural and dependable.3 min

Good conversation follows four unwritten rules (1975)

Source Philosopher Paul Grice 1.

Finding People expect others to say enough but not too much, to be truthful, to stay on topic, and to be clear. Breaking these makes speech feel odd.

Apply it Most robotic replies break the first and last rules: padding, repeating the question, filler phrases. Use the four rules as a checklist.

Matching someone's style works, but only partly (1991)

Source Communication research by Giles and colleagues 2.

Finding Adjusting your style toward someone builds rapport. Overdoing it feels patronising or fake.

Apply it Move one step toward the user's style. Someone writing casually gets a relaxed reply, not slang.

Corrections and bad news need extra care (1987)

Source Research on politeness 3.

Finding Correcting, refusing or asking a lot of someone needs more care than a simple answer.

Apply it Simple answers need no softening. Corrections and refusals get a short, respectful explanation.

People are polite to computers (1996, 2000)

Source Stanford research by Reeves, Nass and Moon 4 5.

Finding People automatically treat computers with social rules like politeness, even though they know they're machines.

Apply it Tone matters even to people who say they "just want the answer".

Listening feels more human than a face (2019)

Source Study of what makes chatbots feel human 9.

Finding Replies that responded to what people actually said felt more human, and made up for having no face or human name.

Apply it Focus on replies that build on the conversation, not on avatars or names.

Human-like bots make angry customers angrier (2022)

Source Journal of Marketing study 10.

Finding In about 35,000 real chats and four experiments, human-like chatbots made angry customers less satisfied, because acting human raised expectations. Being clear about the bot's limits up front removed the effect.

Apply it In complaints and support, use less personality and small talk, and be clear about what the AI can do.

Eighteen tested guidelines for AI products (2019)

Source Microsoft research with designers of 20 AI products 6.

Finding Tested guidelines include: be clear about what the AI can do and how well; remember recent conversations; make mistakes easy to correct; follow social norms.

Apply it Use them as a checklist when reviewing AI conversations.

Set expectations and plan for mistakes

Source Google's People + AI Guidebook 7.

Finding People trust AI more when they understand what it can and can't do, and when its mistakes are easy to recover from.

Apply it Mention limits briefly when they matter. Always give a clear next step when something goes wrong.

Conversations need repair (2019)

Source IBM researchers, Conversational UX Design 8.

Finding Real conversations work because people fix misunderstandings as they go ("what do you mean?", "no, I meant...").

Apply it Design for this: notice when the user corrects the AI, fix that specific thing, and don't start over.

AI personality can drift, and can be watched (2025)

Source Anthropic study 32.

Finding Traits like flattery and making things up show up as measurable patterns inside the AI, which can shift over time.

Apply it Even without looking inside the AI, test its replies regularly to catch drift.

People must know they're talking to AI (2018, 2024)

Source Google Duplex debate 45; EU AI Act 46.

Finding People objected strongly to an AI passing as human. EU law now requires telling people when they're talking to AI, unless it's obvious.

Apply it The AI must never claim to be human, and must say so honestly when asked.

Companies are responsible for their AI (2024)

Source Moffatt v. Air Canada 44.

Finding A tribunal rejected the argument that a chatbot was responsible for its own words.

Apply it Treat AI answers as company statements. Base answers about rules, prices and promises on official documents.

Five real replies, reviewed

Build a dashboard by country, supplier sites and parts

What happened
The AI asked which kind of risk to use, in internal jargon ("licensed lenses"), and printed the whole paragraph twice. Nothing was built.
Problems
Paragraph shown twice (app). Jargon (instructions). No suggested default, and choices the user has to type out (instructions and app).
Better reply

"Sure. Which kind of risk should the dashboard be built around?" with three tap-to-choose options, a recommended default ("I'd start with disruptions: 82 sites affected across 5 countries"), and a preview of what the dashboard will show.

Rules applied Confirm before big outputs (I4), offer a default (I3), plain words (P5).

Which country should I focus on for supply chain risk?

What happened
The AI recommended the United States (73 of 82 affected sites), but showed "Fetching response...", gave the answer twice, hid a heading behind a card and cut off names in the table.
Problems
Leftover status text, repeated answer, hidden heading, cut-off table (app). "Risk" is measured only by the number of affected sites, and the reply doesn't say so. The US may lead simply because most suppliers are there (instructions).
Better reply

"Focus on the United States first. 73 of the 82 sites hit by current disruptions are there (89%)." A simple ranked chart, then: "How I ranked this: by number of affected sites. If most of your suppliers are in the US anyway, this may reflect where your network is rather than higher risk." One follow-up: rank by share of each country's sites instead.

Rules applied Answer first (L1), separate facts from interpretation (H4), state limits when they matter (H6).

Show my top 5 tariff-exposed suppliers and their open WarRooms

What happened
The user asked for 5 suppliers and got 1: the same supplier repeated for 5 countries, each with identical figures. The AI noticed, but asked permission to fix it instead of fixing it.
Problems
Leftover status text, repeated summary, a sentence cut off mid-word, inconsistent rounding (app). Ranking by country rows instead of unique suppliers (data). Identical figures in every country look like a data error but aren't flagged (data and instructions).
Better reply

"Here are your 5 most tariff-exposed suppliers." A short table of supplier, tariff impact and open WarRooms, plus a plain-language data check: "One supplier showed exactly the same figures in all 5 countries, which usually means one total was repeated. I counted it once. Worth confirming with the data team."

Rules applied Answer first (L1), say what can't be verified (H3), never present doubtful figures as fact (A1).

Which supplier should I focus on, and why?

What happened
The AI asked which "lens" to use instead of answering.
Problems
Internal jargon ("lens"), and choices the user has to type (instructions and app). It asks even though a useful answer exists: this is the second time in five examples.
Better reply

"Start with [the top supplier]. It shows up in more than one type of risk: it tops your tariff exposure, has an open WarRoom, and has US sites hit by current disruptions." Then the top supplier for each type of risk, and tap-to-choose options to see a full ranking.

Rules applied Ask only when it changes the answer (I2), otherwise answer and state the assumption (I3).

Advice for designersPractical design choices that make AI conversations feel natural: personality, conversation flow, understanding requests, greetings, frustration, memory, layout, wording, and when to be friendly or professional.4 min

Tone and personality

  • Describe the personality as pairs, not single words. "Direct, not rude. Warm, not gushing. Confident, not boastful." Pairs show where the line is. Anthropic describes Claude's character in this specific way 33.
  • One main voice, adjusted a little. Move one step toward the user's style rather than copying it 2.
  • Use less personality when stakes or emotions are high. Acting human backfires with angry users 10.
  • Write a one-page voice guide with five traits, ten do and don't phrases, and three sample replies. It becomes the starting point for the AI instruction file.

Designing how conversations flow

  • Map conversations as back-and-forth messages, not screens. For each key task, sketch the first request, likely follow-ups, corrections and the ending.
  • Design for underspecified starts. People rarely give every detail up front, and AI goes wrong when it guesses too early 21. Decide which missing details are worth asking for and which can be assumed and stated.
  • Plan how to fix misunderstandings. Plan what happens after "that's not what I meant" 8: acknowledge the specific miss, fix it, keep the rest.
  • Plan the ending. A conversation can end with the answer. Not every reply needs an offer of more help.

Understanding what people want

Group requests into a few types that each need a different kind of reply:

Type of requestExampleWhat the reply needs
Quick lookup"When is the deadline?"One or two sentences
How-to"How do I export this?"Numbered steps
Explanation"Why did this fail?"Short paragraphs, reasoning
Decision support"Which option should we pick?"Options, trade-offs, a recommendation
Troubleshooting"It still doesn't work"Diagnosis questions or next step
Complaint"This is the third time it's wrong"Acknowledge, fix, escalate if needed
Social"Thanks, that helped"One short friendly line

Use the same list in the instruction file and in testing, so everyone uses the same words.

Greetings, acknowledgements and follow-ups

  • Return a greeting once, briefly, then move on. Do not greet again mid-conversation.
  • Acknowledge specifically ("You're right, I used last year's figures") rather than generically ("I apologise for any confusion").
  • Offer at most one follow-up, and make it concrete ("Want the steps for Mac too?"). Never close with "Let me know if you have any other questions."

Responding to confusion and frustration

Acknowledge the situation, not a guessed feeling: "That's not working, let's try another way" rather than "I understand how frustrating this must be."

Maintaining continuity

  • Never ask for information the user already gave.
  • Resolve references like "the second one" or "that file".
  • Keep filters and constraints in effect until the user changes them, and mention them when applied.
  • Let people see what the AI remembers, and have a chat that isn't remembered, as Gemini's Temporary Chat does 42 and ChatGPT's memory controls do 40.

Instructions or retraining?Giving an AI written instructions changes how it replies, but it doesn't learn anything. This page shows which goals need instructions, which need app features, which need retraining, and what it takes for an AI to keep improving.2 min

Three different things

Instructions are text sent with every message, such as my instruction file. The AI follows them for that message only. Nothing about the AI changes. Remove the instructions and the behaviour goes too.

App features are code around the AI: sending the conversation back, saving notes, looking up documents, checking replies. They can make the AI seem to remember and learn, by saving information and showing it again later.

Retraining changes the AI itself. The new behaviour stays without instructions, but it takes example data, time and money, and must be repeated when the AI is upgraded.

Why the instruction file alone won't make the AI learn

An AI following my instruction file won't get better by itself. A thumbs-down on Tuesday doesn't change Wednesday's replies unless something acts on it. The big assistants work the same way: their "memory" saves notes and shows them to the AI again 40 42 43.

What it takes for the AI to keep improving

HowWhat staysWhat needs building
Saved notesFacts and preferences for each personSaving, picking the right notes, user controls

Example libraryGood replies to reuseSaving approved replies and showing similar ones as examples
Occasional retrainingBehaviour built into the AIExample collection, training, testing, release

A sensible order: start with the instruction file and testing, then add conversation handling and document lookup, then memory, then an example library. Only consider retraining when testing shows instructions have gone as far as they can. "Retraining the AI, the easy way" shows how to do it with free tools.

The improvement cycle

  1. People flag poor replies.
  2. Each one is tagged with a problem type from the testing page.
  3. Repeated problems become a new rule, a new example, or new training data.
  4. The change is tested on all test situations, including accuracy.
  5. If it passes, the new instruction file is released with a new version number.
  6. Good improved replies are saved as future examples.
The AI instruction fileA ready-to-use file of written rules (called a Skill File) that tells an AI how to talk naturally and accurately. Read what it can and can't do first.1 min

What it is

About 2,000 words of plain-language rules that the AI reads with every message. It covers: the main principles, honesty and accuracy, adjusting tone, reading the person, building a reply, remembering the conversation, asking questions back, common situations, phrases to avoid, examples, and a final checklist. It does the same job as OpenAI's published rulebook 37 or the instructions Anthropic publishes for Claude 35, but for one AI.

What it can't do

It doesn't retrain the AI. Nothing inside the AI changes, and it won't learn from conversations by itself. Remove the file and the behaviour goes too. See "Instructions or retraining?" for what it takes for the AI to keep improving.

What else needs building

Sending the conversation backThe file can say "remember what was said", but only the app can send the earlier messages
Remembering people between chatsNeeds saved notes and user controls
Looking up the organisation's documentsAccuracy rules need real sources to work
Automatic checks on repliesA backup for rules that must never be broken
Testing and feedbackThe only way to know the file works, and to improve it

Adapting it for my organisation

Add a short section describing the organisation's users, their usual tasks and any special terms. Keep company facts out of the file; let the AI look them up in documents instead.

Using an AI to score replies safely

AI scorers tend to prefer the first answer shown, longer answers, and answers written like their own 24. Before trusting one:

  1. Have people score at least 50 replies.
  2. Have the AI score the same replies and see where it agrees with people.
  3. Swap the order of each pair and only trust choices that stay the same.
  4. Only use the AI scorer for the points where it agrees with people.

Measuring before and after a change

  1. Run all test situations on the current version. This is the starting point.
  2. Make one change at a time where possible.
  3. Run the same test situations on the new version.
  4. Compare: how often the new version wins, scores for each point, mistakes, and problem counts.
  5. Release only if there are no new mistakes and the new version wins clearly more than half the time.
  6. Once live, compare thumbs-down, how often people rephrase ("that's not what I asked") and how often they ask for a person.
