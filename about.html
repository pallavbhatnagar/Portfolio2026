How I got here

I started in graphic design, drawn to the way a good layout could make people feel calm. Then I watched users struggle with a checkout I had made beautiful, and I learned that polish is not the same as clarity.

Since then I've worked across fintech, healthcare, logistics and education, always close to the people using the product. I run the research, shape the flows, design the interface and often build the prototype myself, with AI and code as part of my everyday toolkit.

The work I'm proudest of is rarely the loudest: the onboarding that stopped support tickets, the dashboard a dispatcher can read in two seconds, the design system that lets a team move without asking permission.

Working with me

Who you'd be working with🤝

A designer who builds, and a teammate who makes the work easier for everyone around it.

The short version: calm in messy projects, clear in handoff, and honest about what we don't know yet.

Built alongside engineers, PMs and researchers

Where I start

With the problem, not the pixels.

Before any screen, I talk to the people who use the product and write down what is really getting in their way. The design follows from that.

How I collaborate

I design in the open.

Rough work goes up early, so engineers and product can shape it while it is still cheap to change.

After handoff

I stay until it ships.

I answer questions fast during the build and check the live version against the design before calling it done.

Teams usually bring me in to

  • Take a design from Figma all the way to shipped code.

Life, lately

Trips, weekends and whatever has my attention lately.

Questions people ask?

Short answers to what teams usually ask me first.

Are you open to new roles?

Yes. I'm looking for senior product design roles on teams that ship often and talk to their users. Product companies interest me most, especially ones where the product is complex and the people using it are busy: finance, health, logistics, B2B tools.

What matters more than the industry is the setup: a team that gives design a seat early, measures what it ships, and is honest about what isn't working yet.

I can start in about 30 days. Happy to do a portfolio walkthrough, a design exercise or a short paid trial first, whichever helps you decide.

Remote or on-site?

Either works. I'm based in Bengaluru and have worked with teams across India, Europe and the US, so I'm used to splitting the day around other time zones.

Remotely, I keep at least a few hours of overlap with the team, write decisions down so nobody has to wait on a call, and share work in progress in the open rather than saving it for a big reveal.

In person, I'm happy to be in the office regularly, and I think kickoffs, research sessions and workshops are worth travelling for wherever the team is.

What does your process look like on a new project?

I start by listening: to users, to support tickets, to sales calls and to the team. In the first week or two I want to understand who the product is for, where they get stuck, and what the business needs from the fix.

That becomes a one-page brief: the problem in plain words, who it affects, what we'll change and how we'll know it worked. Everyone signs off on that before anyone opens Figma.

Then I sketch rough flows, test them early with real people, and only polish what survives. Expect clickable prototypes within the first couple of weeks, not just slides, and a design that's been argued with by engineering before it's final.

Do you code?

Enough to prototype in HTML, CSS and JavaScript, and to talk details with engineers. I built this site myself, from the layout to the little dog.

I don't ship production code, but I can read a pull request, check the build against the design, and suggest the CSS fix instead of just filing a bug.

Prototyping in code also changes how I design: I find edge cases sooner, I respect what's expensive to build, and I can test motion and real data instead of guessing at them.

How do you use AI in your work?

As a fast assistant, not a designer. I use it to summarise research notes, cluster feedback, draft first versions of microcopy, and build quick prototypes I can put in front of people sooner.

It saves hours on the busywork, which leaves more time for the parts that need judgement: deciding what matters, talking to users and making the trade-offs.

Every decision still goes back to real users and the team. And I'm careful with data: nothing confidential goes into an AI tool unless the company has approved it.
